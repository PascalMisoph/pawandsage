**Quick answer:** It is not a good treat for cats. VCA Animal Hospitals lists **any food with xylitol, "such as some peanut butters,"** among foods to avoid, and the ASPCA warns that **nuts are high in oils and fats** that can cause vomiting, diarrhea and potentially pancreatitis.

## Check for xylitol first

Xylitol is a sugar substitute used in gum, candy, baked goods, toothpaste and some nut butters. The ASPCA says xylitol can cause **low blood sugar and potentially liver damage**, depending on the amount, with early signs such as vomiting, lethargy and loss of coordination that can progress to seizures. If your cat ate anything containing xylitol, call your vet or poison control right away.

## Even plain peanut butter is a poor fit

Cornell Feline Health Center notes cats are obligate carnivores, and peanut butter is a dense, fatty food. The ASPCA notes that nuts contain high amounts of oils and fats that can cause vomiting and diarrhea, and potentially pancreatitis in some pets. Peanut butter is also sticky and thick; WSAVA advises choosing treats of an appropriate size and shape to reduce choking risk.

If you want to hide a pill, ask your veterinarian about pill pockets made for cats or compounded medication.

## Better treat options

VCA lists foods such as cooked meats, cooked eggs and seedless watermelon as safe treats when offered plain, and recommends low-calorie choices. See [can cats eat scrambled eggs?](/blog/can-cats-eat-scrambled-eggs) and [can cats eat watermelon?](/blog/can-cats-eat-watermelon)

## How much is "a little"?

Human food counts toward your cat's treat budget. VCA Animal Hospitals says treats and snacks should make up no more than **10% of daily calories**, Cornell Feline Health Center gives a rule of thumb of **10 to 15%**, and WSAVA says **under 10%**. WSAVA's own table puts that at roughly **18 to 28 calories a day** for a healthy adult cat weighing 2.5 to 7 kg (about 5.5 to 15 lb). Your veterinarian can tell you what your cat needs.

## When to call the vet

Call your veterinarian if your cat vomits repeatedly, has diarrhea that does not settle, seems lethargic or stops eating, or if the food contained anything toxic such as onion, garlic, chocolate, grapes or xylitol. For a suspected poisoning, call the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center at [(888) 426-4435](tel:+18884264435) (a consultation fee may apply) or Pet Poison Helpline at [(855) 764-7661](tel:+18557647661) (an incident fee applies).
