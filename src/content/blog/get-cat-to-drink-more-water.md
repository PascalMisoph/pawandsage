Cats are terrible at drinking water, and it is not their fault. They descend from desert animals built to extract moisture from prey, so they have a weak thirst drive and tend to run slightly dehydrated even on a normal day. Add summer heat, and that small daily deficit becomes a real risk — for urinary and kidney health especially, both of which depend on a well-hydrated cat. The good news is that most cats will happily drink more; you just have to make the water worth their attention. Here are nine ways to do exactly that.

## 1. Switch to a Fountain

This is the single biggest lever for most cats. In the wild, still water can be stagnant and unsafe, so many cats instinctively prefer moving water and will drink far more from a flowing source.

**What helps:** A circulating [Catit Flower Fountain](https://amazon.com/dp/B0146QXOB0?tag=pawandsage21-20) keeps water aerated, cooler, and more interesting than a bowl that has gone flat by noon. For a lot of reluctant drinkers, a fountain alone solves the problem.

## 2. Put Water Everywhere

Cats drink more when water is convenient and they stumble across it. Place bowls in several rooms, on different levels, so a drink is never more than a few steps away. In summer, more stations means more total intake without any nagging.

## 3. Move Water Away From Food and Litter

In the wild, cats keep their kill and their water apart to avoid contamination, and that instinct survives in your living room. A bowl right beside the food or anywhere near the litter box can quietly put a cat off. Give water its own spot.

## 4. Switch the Bowl Itself

Plastic bowls hold odors and can taint the taste; narrow bowls cause "whisker fatigue," the discomfort of whiskers brushing the sides. Try a wide, shallow ceramic or stainless steel bowl filled to the brim so your cat does not have to dip its face in.

## 5. Lean on Wet Food

Wet food is roughly 70 to 80 percent water, so feeding more of it is the easiest hydration win there is — your cat drinks without knowing it. In a heatwave, shifting the balance toward wet meals does a lot of quiet work. Our guide on [puzzle feeders for wet vs dry food](/blog/cat-puzzle-feeder-wet-food) covers how to serve it well.

## 6. Add Water to Meals

Stir a few tablespoons of warm water or a little cat-safe, unsalted broth into wet food to make a "soup." Most cats lap up the gravy and take in extra fluid with it. Start small so the new texture does not put them off.

## 7. Try Ice Cubes

Some cats are fascinated by ice. Drop a cube or two in the water bowl to keep it cold and give them a moving target to bat and lick. A colder bowl is also simply more appealing on a hot day than lukewarm water.

## 8. Keep It Fresh

Cats turn their noses up at water that has sat out collecting dust and going warm. Refresh bowls at least once or twice a day in summer, more if they sit in a warm room. Fountains help here too, since the circulation keeps water fresher between changes.

## 9. Use a Lick Mat for Watery Treats

Spread a thin layer of wet food, a watery topper, or a cat-safe broth on a [LickiMat](https://amazon.com/dp/B089DN2HY7?tag=pawandsage21-20) and let your cat work it off. It stretches a small amount of moisture into a longer, satisfying session — and doubles as light enrichment on a lazy hot afternoon.

## How to Tell If It's Working

A well-hydrated cat has moist gums, elastic skin that springs back when gently lifted, and produces normal amounts of urine. Watch for warning signs that need a vet: lethargy, sunken eyes, skin that stays "tented" when pinched, or noticeably less urine in the litter box. Cats with kidney or urinary history need extra attention in summer, so loop in your vet if you are concerned.

Hydration is one piece of the warm-weather picture. Pair it with a cool retreat and good airflow — see [how to keep your cat cool in hot weather](/blog/keep-cat-cool-hot-weather) — and watch for the [warning signs of heatstroke](/blog/heatstroke-in-cats), since dehydration and overheating tend to arrive together.
