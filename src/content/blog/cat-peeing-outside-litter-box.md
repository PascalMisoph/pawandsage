**Quick answer:** Rule out a medical problem first, because painful urination is one of the most common reasons cats avoid the box. Then work through the box itself: cleanliness, litter type, size, location and stress. Cornell Feline Health Center says house soiling is **the most common behavior problem cat owners report**, and that the fix depends on the cause: a medical problem, an aversion to the box, or a preference for another spot.

> **When it is an emergency:** a cat that keeps straining in the litter box and passes little or no urine needs a veterinarian **right now**. VCA Animal Hospitals calls a urethral obstruction "an absolute emergency." Cornell Feline Health Center lists male cats among those at increased risk of lower urinary tract disease.

## Step 1: Book a vet check

Cornell explains that any medical condition that interferes with normal urination can cause litter box problems. Inflammation of the urinary tract, for example, makes urinating painful and more frequent, so the cat may start to link the box with pain. Kidney disease, thyroid disease and diabetes can increase urine volume. The ASPCA adds that a cat that keeps entering the box but produces only small amounts of urine may have a urinary tract infection.

The owner guide based on the AAFP and ISFM house-soiling guidelines lists **feline idiopathic cystitis (FIC)** as a frequent cause and notes it can be aggravated by stress. Your vet can check urine and rule these out.

## Step 2: Look at the box through your cat's eyes

Cornell's red flags for **litter box aversion**: your cat cries in the box, won't bury its waste, perches on the edge without touching the litter, or goes right next to the box. Check these:

- **Cleanliness.** Some cats refuse a box with any waste in it. The ASPCA says to scoop **at least once a day**.
- **Litter.** Most cats prefer **unscented** litter (Cornell). The AAFP/ISFM-based guide says most prefer soft, unscented clumping litter and many dislike scented or dusty litter and box liners.
- **Depth.** The ASPCA says cats usually prefer **one to two inches** of litter.
- **Size.** Boxes should be **1.5 times the length of your cat**, nose to base of tail; many commercial boxes are too small (AAFP/ISFM-based guide).
- **Covers.** Cornell notes that covers hold odors in from the cat's point of view. The ASPCA lists hoods and liners among common reasons cats avoid boxes.

If your cat pees over the edge while standing, a box with higher sides can help; see [litter boxes for cats that pee high](/blog/best-litter-box-for-cats-that-pee-high). For litter types cats tend to accept, see our [best cat litter guide](/blog/best-cat-litter).

## Step 3: Check location and the other pets

The AAFP/ISFM-based guide says cats prefer quiet, private places, away from busy areas and anywhere they can be cornered. Cornell asks whether another animal ambushes your cat in or near the box. Give the box more than one way out and keep boxes in different locations. The usual rule is **one box per cat, plus one**. More on placement in [where to put a litter box](/blog/where-to-put-litter-box) and [how many litter boxes per cat](/blog/how-many-litter-boxes-per-cat).

## Step 4: Clean the accidents properly

Cornell says odors must be **neutralized, not just deodorized**, and warns against ammonia or vinegar cleaners because they smell like urine. The ASPCA recommends an **enzymatic cleaner** designed for pet odors.

## Step 5: Don't punish

Cornell says cats probably lack the cognitive ability for "revenge" and do not see urine as unpleasant the way we do. Punishment adds stress, and stress makes FIC and avoidance worse. If a cat is spraying (backing up to a vertical surface with the tail upright, as the ASPCA describes), that is marking, a different problem. Cornell notes that a synthetic pheromone (Feliway) can discourage spraying on treated areas; we compare the options in [Feliway vs Comfort Zone](/blog/feliway-vs-comfort-zone).
