**Quick answer:** To keep a cat warm in winter, even without heat, give them an enclosed or high-sided bed off the cold floor and out of drafts. Line it with a fleece blanket or a self-warming pad that reflects their body heat. Add a sunny window seat for the day, and keep one unheated spot so they can regulate themselves.

Cats run warmer than we do. PetMD puts normal feline body temperature at about 100.4–102.5°F, which is why they hunt down sunbeams, laptops and warm laundry. A chilly apartment, a drafty old house, a thermostat turned down to save money or a winter power cut are all manageable. You just have to give your cat warm places to sleep.

**Key takeaways**

- **Most cats get uncomfortable below about 45°F.** Seniors, kittens and thin or sick cats can feel it from 50–60°F (PetMD).
- **Fight cold from below and from drafts first.** A raised, covered bed does more than a heater across the room.
- **No-electricity warmth:** self-warming pads, fleece, cat caves, sunny windows and a microwavable heat disc.
- **Always leave a cooler option.** Cats should be able to move away from any heat source.
- **Shivering, weakness or lethargy is not "just cold".** Those are hypothermia signs: call your vet.

## How Cold Is Too Cold for a Cat Indoors?

There's no single magic number, because cold tolerance depends on age, coat, body fat and health. PetMD's veterinary guidance gives useful ranges:

| Indoor temperature | What it means for most cats |
|---|---|
| 50–60°F | Cats with weight loss, arthritis or chronic illness may be quite uncomfortable |
| 45–50°F | Small, thin-coated, hairless, senior and young cats feel it |
| 32–45°F | Most healthy adults are uncomfortable if it lasts |
| Below 32°F | Risk of frostbite and hypothermia for all cats with prolonged exposure |

*Source: PetMD, "How Cold Is Too Cold for Cats?" (reviewed by a veterinarian).*

The AVMA adds that cats with diabetes, heart disease, kidney disease or hormonal disorders may have a harder time regulating body temperature. The same goes for very young and very old pets. If you share your home with one of those cats, aim for the top of that table, not the bottom.

## Fix the Floor and the Drafts First

Before you buy anything, check two things: what your cat is lying on, and where the cold air comes from.

1. **Get the bed off the floor.** Tile, concrete and floors over unheated basements pull heat out of a sleeping cat. A bed on a chair, sofa, shelf or cat tree platform is warmer than the same bed on the floor.
2. **Move beds away from windows and exterior doors at night.** Glass and door gaps are where cold air pours in. An interior wall or a bedroom corner works best.
3. **Block drafts cheaply.** Roll a towel against the bottom of a drafty door, close curtains after sunset, and close off rooms you're not heating.
4. **Use the sun during the day.** Open south- or west-facing blinds on sunny afternoons and put a perch or a folded blanket in that patch. A [window perch](/blog/cat-window-perch) turns that sun into a daily heated bed and some cat TV.

## Give Your Cat a Covered "Den"

Cats conserve heat by curling up in a small, enclosed space, and a covered bed helps them trap it. PetMD notes that a covered bed helps cold cats warm up quickly when no warm room is available.

- A **cat cave**, a hooded bed or even a **cardboard box on its side** lined with fleece works.
- Lay a **fleece blanket** inside and another loosely over the top for very cold nights.
- Put one den where your cat already likes to sleep, and a second somewhere quiet.

Give your cat choices. The AVMA recommends offering several comfortable sleeping spots so a pet can move to a warmer or cooler one as needed. That matters even more once you add any heat source.

**Safety rules for anything with a plug:**

- **Use pet-specific, low-wattage products.** Human heating pads and electric blankets aren't designed for a cat who may sleep on them for hours.
- **Cover or route cords** where your cat can't chew them, and check them often.
- **Keep an unheated spot nearby.** The AVMA cautions that heated pet mats can still cause burns. A cat who can move away regulates themselves.
- **Space heaters need extra care.** The AVMA warns they can burn pets or get knocked over and start a fire. Choose a model with a tip-over shut-off, keep it well away from beds and curtains, and never leave it running in a room with an unsupervised cat.

## How to Keep Your Cat Warm at Night

Nights are when houses cool fastest, especially if you lower the thermostat.

- **Put the den in the warmest interior room,** often your bedroom. Many cats choose to sleep near you anyway, so leave the door open.
- **Pre-warm the bed.** Put a heat disc under the blanket an hour before bed, then move it to one end.
- **Play, then feed, before bed.** A short evening play session followed by a meal helps your cat settle and sleep curled up. Our [evening routine for hyper cats](/blog/how-to-tire-out-a-cat) has the details.
- **Keep water fresh and unfrozen.** Heating dries indoor air, and some cats drink less in winter. See [how to get your cat to drink more water](/blog/get-cat-to-drink-more-water) for easy fixes.

## Power Outage? A Simple Winter Plan

The AVMA advises including pets in your emergency plan, with at least 5 days of food, water and medicine. Ready.gov also recommends a pet emergency kit. For cold-weather outages, add:

1. **One "warm room."** Pick a small interior room, close the door and gather everyone there, cats included. Body heat in a small space adds up.
2. **Insulated dens.** Put cat caves or boxes lined with fleece and a self-warming pad on a bed or sofa, not the floor.
3. **Heat discs, charged in advance.** Warm microwavable discs while you still have power. They release heat for hours.
4. **The carrier, ready to go.** If your home gets too cold and you need to leave, take your cat with you. PetMD warns that leaving pets behind in a cold-weather emergency can lead to hypothermia and death. Check ahead which shelters or hotels accept pets.
5. **Carbon monoxide safety.** Never use a grill, camp stove or generator indoors. The AVMA recommends carbon monoxide detectors, and pets are affected too.

## Warning Signs Your Cat Is Too Cold

PetMD notes that cats often hide discomfort and may not shiver or cry until they're quite cold. Watch for:

- **Early signs:** curling into a tight ball, tucking the nose under the tail, seeking vents, laps and laundry, cool ears and paws.
- **Hypothermia signs:** shivering, lethargy, weakness, reduced appetite, slow movement or collapse. Hypothermia means a body temperature below about 100°F.

If you see hypothermia signs, warm your cat gradually with blankets and a covered bed, and call your vet right away. Don't put a cold cat directly on a hot pad.

## Indoor Cats in Winter: Don't Forget Their Minds

Short, dark days often mean less play and more sleep. Some extra napping in winter is normal, but a cat who seems flat, hides more or eats less deserves a closer look.
