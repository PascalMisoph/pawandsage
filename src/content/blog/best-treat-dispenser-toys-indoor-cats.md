Treat dispenser toys work because they make food behave more like prey.

Instead of a snack appearing in a bowl, your cat has to sniff, paw, bat, wait, and solve. That small amount of effort can turn a bored afternoon into a satisfying hunting task.

The best toy is not the most complicated one. It is the one your cat can solve without quitting.

## Start With Easy Wins

Begin with a simple rolling treat ball or beginner puzzle. If the toy is too hard, your cat learns that puzzles are frustrating. If it is too easy, it becomes a bowl with extra steps.

Use part of a meal so the calories are already accounted for.

## Add Movement Carefully

## Match the Toy to the Boredom Pattern

For fast eaters, use rolling balls or maze feeders.

For cats who pace, use a small room treat hunt.

For cats who paw at doors or curtains, try vertical or doorway-based puzzles under supervision.

For anxious cats, use quieter food puzzles and lick mats.

For the full brain-work menu, read [mental stimulation for cats](/blog/mental-stimulation-for-cats).

## Safety Rules

Do not leave out toys with strings, elastic, loose feathers, bells that can detach, or small parts unless you know your cat uses them safely. Even then, inspect regularly.

Also avoid creating a toy that only works when your cat is frantic. Food puzzles should lower boredom, not wind the cat up until they are crashing around the room.

## The Best Routine

Use treat dispenser toys at predictable times:

- After morning play.
- During your work block.
- In the hot afternoon when chase play is too much.
- Before dinner as a small hunting task.

Rotate them so they stay interesting. A toy left out forever becomes furniture. A toy that appears for ten focused minutes can stay valuable for months.
