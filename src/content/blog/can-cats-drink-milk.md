**Quick answer:** It is not recommended. Cornell Feline Health Center says milk is **not generally recommended as a treat for cats** because **many cats are lactose intolerant** and can develop gastrointestinal problems. The ASPCA says the same about dairy: it can cause **diarrhea or other digestive upset**.

## The saucer-of-milk myth

The image of a cat lapping milk is a cultural classic, not veterinary advice. The ASPCA explains that pets lack significant amounts of **lactase**, the enzyme that breaks down lactose. VCA Animal Hospitals says most cats are lactose intolerant, and that kittens eating dairy can experience gastrointestinal upset.

WSAVA also counts milk toward your cat's treat calories: about **27 calories in 2 fl oz (59 ml) of semi-skimmed cow's milk**, roughly an entire day's treat allowance for many adult cats.

## What about kittens?

If you are caring for an orphaned or very young kitten, call your veterinarian for advice on a kitten milk replacer and a feeding plan; see our [new kitten checklist](/blog/new-kitten-checklist) for first-week basics.

## What to offer instead

Fresh, clean water. If you are worried your cat does not drink enough, ask your veterinarian.

If your cat drank some milk and now has diarrhea, it will often settle, but call your vet if it lasts, if there is vomiting or blood, or if your cat is a kitten, a senior or has other health problems.

## How much is "a little"?

Human food counts toward your cat's treat budget. VCA Animal Hospitals says treats and snacks should make up no more than **10% of daily calories**, Cornell Feline Health Center gives a rule of thumb of **10 to 15%**, and WSAVA says **under 10%**. WSAVA's own table puts that at roughly **18 to 28 calories a day** for a healthy adult cat weighing 2.5 to 7 kg (about 5.5 to 15 lb). Your veterinarian can tell you what your cat needs.

## When to call the vet

Call your veterinarian if your cat vomits repeatedly, has diarrhea that does not settle, seems lethargic or stops eating, or if the food contained anything toxic such as onion, garlic, chocolate, grapes or xylitol. For a suspected poisoning, call the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center at [(888) 426-4435](tel:+18884264435) (a consultation fee may apply) or Pet Poison Helpline at [(855) 764-7661](tel:+18557647661) (an incident fee applies).
