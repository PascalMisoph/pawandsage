**Quick answer:** Only as a rare, small treat, never as a regular food. VCA Animal Hospitals warns that **regularly feeding tuna is dangerous because of its mercury content**, and Cornell Feline Health Center notes that some cats fed **canned fish meant for humans** have developed **potentially serious neurological disorders**.

## Why tuna is not a meal

VCA Animal Hospitals says human diets are not nutritionally sound for cats, and warns that some kittens will hold out for treats like tuna and refuse their balanced cat food.

If you do share, VCA lists **cooked seafood without seasonings** among foods cats can enjoy. Pick tuna **packed in water**, not oil or brine, with no added salt, garlic or onion, and give a small flake or two.

## The calories

WSAVA's treat guide lists **canned tuna in water at about 36 calories per ounce (28 g)**. That is more than the entire daily treat allowance (about 18 to 28 calories) for many adult cats.

## What about tuna-flavored cat food?

Commercial cat foods that contain tuna are formulated as part of a complete diet, which is different from sharing your sandwich tuna. If you have questions about how much fish is in your cat's diet, ask your veterinarian.

## Never raw

Cornell says raw meat is not recommended for cats because it can carry toxoplasmosis and other infectious diseases, and the ASPCA warns raw meat can contain Salmonella and E. coli.

## How much is "a little"?

Human food counts toward your cat's treat budget. VCA Animal Hospitals says treats and snacks should make up no more than **10% of daily calories**, Cornell Feline Health Center gives a rule of thumb of **10 to 15%**, and WSAVA says **under 10%**. WSAVA's own table puts that at roughly **18 to 28 calories a day** for a healthy adult cat weighing 2.5 to 7 kg (about 5.5 to 15 lb). Your veterinarian can tell you what your cat needs.

## When to call the vet

Call your veterinarian if your cat vomits repeatedly, has diarrhea that does not settle, seems lethargic or stops eating, or if the food contained anything toxic such as onion, garlic, chocolate, grapes or xylitol. For a suspected poisoning, call the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center at [(888) 426-4435](tel:+18884264435) (a consultation fee may apply) or Pet Poison Helpline at [(855) 764-7661](tel:+18557647661) (an incident fee applies).
