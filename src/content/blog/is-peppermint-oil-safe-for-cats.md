**Quick answer:** No. VCA Animal Hospitals lists **peppermint oil** among essential oils that are **poisonous to cats**, through both ingestion and skin exposure. The ASPCA also lists **mint plants** (*Mentha*, garden mint) as **toxic to cats**, with **vomiting and diarrhea with large ingestions**.

## Mint plants

The ASPCA lists the toxic principle in garden mint as its essential oils. The listed signs (vomiting and diarrhea) are tied to **large ingestions**, so a single nibble is less concerning than a cat that grazes a pot of mint. Still, keep mint out of reach and away from cat-safe herbs like [basil](/blog/is-basil-safe-for-cats) and [rosemary](/blog/is-rosemary-safe-for-cats), which are non-toxic on the ASPCA list.

Don't confuse mint with catnip. Catnip (*Nepeta cataria*, sometimes called catmint) is a different plant in the same family.

## Peppermint oil, sprays and "natural" pest repellents

Peppermint oil turns up in cleaning sprays, "natural" pest repellents, cooling rubs and diffuser blends. Texas A&M's veterinary college includes mint (including wintergreen, spearmint and peppermint) on its list of essential oils toxic to pets. Wintergreen also appears on VCA's list of oils poisonous to cats.

Spraying peppermint oil around the house to keep pests or cats off counters puts concentrated oil exactly where your cat walks, lies and grooms. Use cat-safe ways to redirect your cat instead, such as giving it a legal high perch.

## Why cats are more sensitive than dogs or people

The Merck Veterinary Manual explains that cats are more sensitive to essential oils because they are **deficient in the liver enzyme glucuronyl transferase**, which makes them very sensitive to phenols and phenolic compounds some oils contain. Their grooming habit adds risk: oil on the fur ends up swallowed. VCA adds that both ingestion and skin exposure can be toxic and that these chemicals are rapidly absorbed.

## Signs of essential oil poisoning in cats

VCA Animal Hospitals lists these signs of essential oil poisoning in cats:

- Scent of the oil on the coat, skin, breath or vomit
- Drooling, pawing at the mouth or face
- Vomiting
- Difficulty walking or an uncoordinated gait
- Lethargy or weakness, muscle tremors
- Difficulty breathing
- Redness or burns on the lips, gums, tongue or skin

The Merck Veterinary Manual adds that signs can develop within minutes to hours, and that severity depends on the type and concentration of the oil and the route of exposure.

## What to do if your cat was exposed

1. **Call right away:** your veterinarian, the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center at [(888) 426-4435](tel:+18884264435) (a consultation fee may apply), or Pet Poison Helpline at [(855) 764-7661](tel:+18557647661) (an incident fee applies; the helpline says it is available 24/7).
2. **Have the details ready:** the product or food, how much and when, and your cat's weight and health conditions. Keep the packaging.
3. **Do not make your cat vomit** and do not give activated charcoal unless a veterinarian tells you to. VCA Animal Hospitals warns it may worsen your cat's condition, and the Merck Veterinary Manual notes the risk of aspiration with essential oils.
4. **If oil is on the fur or skin,** VCA advises washing it off quickly with liquid dishwashing detergent, then calling for advice. Don't let your cat groom it off first.
5. **Get your cat to fresh air** if the exposure was from a diffuser or spill, and put the product packaging in a sealed container to take to the clinic.

## Diffusers: what the experts actually say

- **ASPCA Poison Control:** using a diffuser for a short time in a secured area your cat cannot access is not likely to be an issue. If your cat has a history of breathing problems, avoid diffusers altogether.
- **Merck Veterinary Manual:** keep pets out of the room while a diffuser runs and ventilate afterward; run diffusers only for short periods; use diluted rather than concentrated oils. Passive diffusers (reed diffusers, candles) mainly pose a respiratory-irritation risk unless tipped over; active (nebulizing or ultrasonic) diffusers emit microdroplets of oil.
- **Texas A&M veterinary college:** inhaling diffused oils can cause respiratory irritation, with signs such as watery nose and eyes, drooling, vomiting and difficulty breathing, which in cats may be mistaken for a hairball attempt.

Never apply concentrated essential oils to a cat. Both the ASPCA and Merck say so directly.
